When world leaders adopted the **UN Global Digital Compact (GDC)** as part of the *Pact for the Future*, they set out an ambitious blueprint for an open, free, secure, and human-centric digital future. Yet as anyone working at the intersection of technology and society knows, high-level diplomatic agreements only matter if they translate into lived reality.

Over the past year **Public AI Voice and Engagement (PAVE) Case Book** and the **Citizens’ Track on AI (CTAI)** have been working to bridge the gap between multilateral ambition and grassroots experience. Ahead of the Citizens’ Track featuring today in a Stakeholder Townhall meeting on "[Localizing the Global Digital Compact: From global frameworks to local implementation](https://lnkd.in/p/euEgP5_U)" we have taken the opportunity to reflect on the GDCs core objectives and cross-cutting principles. We realise that PAVE and CTAI are not just aligned with the Compact’s vision, they are actively building the infrastructure required to make it work.

### **Enhancing International AI Governance for the Benefit of Humanity (Objective 5\)**

At the heart of the GDC’s AI commitments is the pledge to govern artificial intelligence for the collective benefit of humanity, emphasising transparency, accountability, and robust human oversight. Crucially, the Compact established two landmark mechanisms: the **Global Dialogue on AI Governance** and the **Independent International Scientific Panel on AI (IISP)**.

However, global governance faces a dual challenge: AI systems and regulations are often opaque to everyday citizens, while lived community experiences are rarely legible to formal scientific panels and summit negotiators.

PAVE directly tackles this second deficit. Its Case Book gathers **84 case studies** across **100 countries** (representing over **215,670 participants** and **451,973 hours of public input**). In early 2027, we will synthesise this evidence base specifically to feed into the **IISP’s timeline ahead of Geneva 27** to translate community-based expertise into the foundations for rigorous, actionable policy insights helping to ensure that the UN’s scientific assessments are grounded in lived reality.

### **Closing Digital and Epistemic Divides (Objective 1 & Cross-Cutting Principles)**

The GDC’s very first objective is to **close all digital divides**, underpinned by the principle that *inclusive participation of all States and stakeholders is the cornerstone of digital cooperation*, with targeted support for developing countries and the Global Majority.

Too often, marginalised communities, particularly across the Global Majority, are treated as passive consumers of AI systems designed elsewhere, rather than active co-governors. PAVE’s current autumn and winter 2026 work program is explicitly designed to counter these systemic power asymmetries. We’ll be working through targeted data sprints, network snowballing, and regional partnerships. We are planning engagements around the Internet Governance Forum (IGF) in Nairobi to actively expand the evidence base from currently under-represented contexts, particularly global majority countries. 

**Advancing Responsible, Equitable, and Interoperable Data Governance (Objective 4\)**

The GDC calls for **responsible, equitable, and interoperable data governance approaches**, recognising that safe, open, and reusable data systems are essential for evidence-based policymaking and public benefit.

PAVE will operate as an open **data commons** with a governance structure design with expert advice from our partners at Aapti Institute. Rather than letting valuable public engagement exercises disappear into disconnected PDF reports, PAVE standardises and maps participatory AI initiatives from around the world. By applying an inclusive, participatory framework for describing evidence rigour, and scaling our dataset from processes covering over 200,000 participants toward the millions, we are creating a shared, interoperable resource. Furthermore, in keeping with open science and digital public goods principles, we are working towards our datasets and research outputs being in public repositories under open licences for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers worldwide. 

### **Embedding Human Rights, Accessibility, and Cultural Diversity**

Finally, the GDC is anchored in international human rights law, emphasising that digital cooperation must respect **linguistic and cultural diversity**, promote **digital accessibility for all**, and address existing concentrations of power. We are working through “North-South, South-South and triangular cooperation” to develop PAVE as data commons of local experiences that reflects linguistic and cultural diversity, respects data privacy and represents local voices. This is an additional challenge, and one we are achieving collectively through consultation with [Aapti Institute](https://aapti.in/). 

In our next phase we plan to interrogate where power asymmetries persist in AI knowledge production. 

### **Looking Ahead**

The Global Digital Compact gave the international community a shared vocabulary for a fairer digital future. Projects like PAVE and the Citizens’ Track on AI provide the democratic muscle to achieve it. True legitimacy in global AI governance cannot be declared from a podium; it has to be built collectively from the ground up, one community voice at a time.
